# Zion Tech Hub: Conclusion & Recommendations

This document answers the three questions from the Data Challenge brief directly, then lays out the full ad budget allocation and growth recommendations those answers are built on. For the underlying methodology, findings, and every data-cleaning decision behind these numbers, see `findings.md`.

---

## Direct Answers to the Brief

### 1. Where should we put our ad budget, and on whom?

**Channel split:** 55% X (Twitter), 30% LinkedIn, 10% Facebook/Instagram test, 5% referral incentive, with no budget behind WhatsApp as an acquisition channel. The full reasoning and table are below.

**Who to target:** X (Twitter) reaches a broad, mixed audience, not concentrated in one profession, so its ad creative should speak to career changers and skill builders generally. Among Cohort 10's occupation categories, Students/Graduates (22.8%), Tech/Data professionals (13.2%), and Business/Entrepreneurship (11.4%) together make up 45.4% of the cohort, the largest identifiable segments, and are consistent with X's own occupation spread being led by Students/recent graduates at 20.5%. LinkedIn's audience leans differently: registrants there are nearly twice as likely to choose Healthcare Data Analytics as X's registrants are (37.3% vs 19.1%), so LinkedIn's ad creative should lean into that course specifically rather than use a generic message. For international targeting, South Africa, Ghana, Kenya, and Uganda are the four markets already showing consistent organic interest across two cohorts, so ad targeting should start there rather than spreading thin.

**Timing:** both cohorts show heavy registration clustering close to the deadline (41.8% of Cohort 9 in a 2-day window 11 days before close; 74.4% of Cohort 10 in a single Thursday/Friday). Budget should be weighted toward a deliberate final-week push rather than spread evenly across the registration window.

### 2. What should we do differently to grow the community and fill Cohort 11?

Seven specific moves, covered in full below: clarify course selection upfront, investigate the WhatsApp decline, plan a deliberate deadline push, build a referral program on LinkedIn's base, look into the Healthcare Data Analytics decline, treat international growth as an X/LinkedIn play rather than WhatsApp, and lean into the founder's personal LinkedIn brand.

### 3. Where and how should we grow organically outside Nigeria?

**The specific market to lean into next: Kenya.**

Country registrations across both cohorts:

| Country | Cohort 9 | Share | Cohort 10 | Share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nigeria | 621 | 56.3% | 103 | 61.3% |
| South Africa | 106 | 9.6% | 11 | 6.5% |
| Ghana | 106 | 9.6% | 9 | 5.4% |
| Kenya | 91 | 8.2% | 20 | 11.9% |
| Uganda | 30 | 2.7% | 6 | 3.6% |

South Africa and Ghana were tied as the strongest international markets in Cohort 9, but both fell sharply by Cohort 10, Ghana in particular dropped from a tie for first among international markets to nearly last. Kenya moved the opposite direction, from third place in Cohort 9 to the clear leading international market in Cohort 10, nearly double South Africa's share and more than double Ghana's. Of the four consistent international markets, Kenya is the only one that grew in both absolute registrants and share of cohort.

What would move the needle: the channel data already answers this. X (Twitter) and LinkedIn, not WhatsApp, are what reach registrants outside Nigeria, X (Twitter) alone reached 72.9% of international registrants in Cohort 9, while WhatsApp reached only 7.7% of them. The same 55/30 channel weighting from Question 1 applies directly to a Kenya-focused push, no new channel strategy is needed, just geographic targeting layered onto the existing plan. Beyond ads, the referral program recommended below is worth piloting specifically among Kenyan LinkedIn registrants first, since a stable channel in a growing market is where referral loops compound fastest.

## Ad Budget Allocation

Assuming a first ad budget with no prior history to lean on, here is how I'd split it, and why.

| Channel | Budget Share | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| X (Twitter) | 55% | The only channel showing genuine, spike-adjusted organic growth (39.6% to 62.8% across cohorts). Also the strongest channel for international reach (72.9% to 86.2% of international registrants used it). Broad audience, not tied to one profession, so ad creative can stay general rather than niche. |
| LinkedIn | 30% | The most stable, predictable channel across both cohorts (24.8% to 23.3%, barely moved). Disproportionately strong for Healthcare Data Analytics registrants. If the goal is reliability rather than reach, this is where it comes from. |
| Test budget (Facebook/Instagram reactivation) | 10% | Both channels were present at low levels in Cohort 9 and vanished in Cohort 10. Rather than writing them off completely on two data points, a small test budget answers the question cheaply: was this a real decline, or did Zion Tech Hub simply stop posting there? |
| Referral incentive | 5% | Referral has stayed small but present in both cohorts (2.4% each), which suggests some natural word of mouth already exists. This isn't ad spend in the traditional sense, it's a small incentive budget (a discount, a bonus, a shoutout) to actively encourage the referrals that are already happening quietly. |

What I'd deliberately not do with a first budget: put money behind WhatsApp. It's the fastest declining channel in the data (28.9% to 7.0%, spike-adjusted) and reaches almost no one outside Nigeria. That doesn't mean abandon WhatsApp altogether, it likely still matters as a community and support tool, just not as a channel worth paying to acquire new registrants through right now.

On targeting: X (Twitter)'s occupation spread is broad, not concentrated in one profession, so ad copy here should speak to career changers and skill builders generally, not one job title. LinkedIn's audience already leans Healthcare, so ad creative and course promotion there should lean into that strength rather than trying to force a generic message. For international reach specifically, South Africa, Ghana, Kenya, and Uganda are the four markets already showing consistent organic interest across two cohorts, so any international ad targeting should start there rather than spreading thin across many countries with no track record.

## Growth Recommendations for Cohort 11

**1. Make course selection clearer before someone commits, to cut down on registration switching.**
Nearly two thirds of people who registered more than once (24 of 37) chose a different course the second time around. That's not someone fixing a typo. That's someone reconsidering. It suggests people aren't always confident about which course fits them the first time they land on the form, which becomes one of the more actionable findings here. A short "which course fits you" guide, or clearer course descriptions on the registration form itself, could reduce this back and forth and the extra admin it creates on your end.

**2. Investigate why WhatsApp is declining before deciding what to do about it.**
A drop from 28.9% to 7.0% is too steep to ignore, but the data can't tell us why on its own; it could be community fatigue, a change in how actively it's being promoted, or something else entirely. This is worth a direct conversation with whoever manages that channel before either doubling down or walking away from it.

**3. Plan a deliberate, timed push near the end of each cohort's registration window, not just an always-on campaign.**
Both cohorts show the same shape: a huge concentration of registrations in a short window close to the deadline (42% of Cohort 9 in a 2-day window 11 days before close; 74% of Cohort 10 in a single Thursday/Friday). Whether or not this was planned the first time, it clearly works. Building a deliberate "final week" push into the Cohort 11 plan, rather than treating steady spend as the whole strategy, could replicate this intentionally instead of hoping it happens again.

**4. Build a real referral program around your most consistent channel's audience.**
LinkedIn's stability suggests a base of people who trust this program enough to keep coming back to it, cohort after cohort. Referral registrations are already happening organically at a low but steady rate. A structured incentive (even a small one) aimed at past registrants and their networks could turn a passive pattern into an active one.

**5. Look into the Healthcare Data Analytics decline before assuming it will fix itself.**
This course dropped from 24.6% to 17.9% of registrations, a real decline even after accounting for Cohort 10's expanded course catalog. Since LinkedIn (your steadiest channel) is also your strongest source of Healthcare-interested registrants, a continued decline here could eventually mean LinkedIn's registrations shrink too, even if LinkedIn itself isn't the problem. Worth understanding whether this is about the course content, its positioning, or market saturation.

**6. Treat international growth as an X (Twitter) and LinkedIn play, not a WhatsApp one.**
Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana, and Kenya are consistently your top four countries across two cohorts. International registrants use X (Twitter) and LinkedIn at meaningfully higher rates than Nigerian registrants do, and barely touch WhatsApp. If international growth is a genuine goal for Cohort 11, the channels to lean into for that specifically are already visible in the data (see Kenya, above, as the specific market showing the clearest growth).

**7. Lean into the founder's personal brand as a zero-cost growth lever for LinkedIn and Healthcare Data Analytics.**
The data shows LinkedIn is Zion Tech Hub's steadiest channel and disproportionately brings in Healthcare Data Analytics registrants. A likely driver behind this (based on founder context, not the dataset itself) is that the CEO's own professional background and following as a healthcare data analyst gives LinkedIn a natural, credible audience for that specific course. If that's accurate, this isn't just a channel to fund with ads, it's a channel to actively publicize through the founder's own voice: more frequent story-sharing, testimonials, or behind-the-scenes content from the CEO on LinkedIn could compound LinkedIn's existing strength at little to no extra ad spend, while directly counteracting the Healthcare Data Analytics decline flagged above.

## Recommendations for Future Data Collection

Beyond the ad budget and growth recommendations above, several of the cleaning challenges in this project point to specific, fixable gaps in how registration data is collected. Addressing these before Cohort 11 opens would mean future analysis starts from clean data rather than needing this same cleanup work repeated.

**1. Replace free-text Country and Occupation fields with dropdown/select lists.**
This alone would have prevented most of the mess: no more "Ghan," "Niger" vs "Nigeria," phone numbers typed into the wrong field, or the 100+ inconsistent occupation entries in Cohort 10. A dropdown forces valid, consistent values at the point of entry instead of needing to be cleaned after the fact.

**2. Make every field required and consistent across every cohort's form.**
Cohort 9's form was missing the Occupation field entirely, which meant an entire dimension of analysis, who is actually registering, couldn't be done for half the data. Before Cohort 11 opens, lock the form fields so every cohort collects the exact same fields, in the exact same format.

**3. Check for existing registrations before accepting a new one.**
43 duplicate registrations in Cohort 9 alone came from people submitting the form more than once. A simple check by email address, "You've already registered, would you like to update your existing registration instead?", would stop duplicates at the source instead of needing to be resolved afterward.

**4. If phone numbers need to be de-identified for privacy, do it in a way that stays unique.**
The short, de-identified phone numbers in this dataset actually collided between unrelated people. If de-identification is a deliberate privacy step, use a method that preserves uniqueness (like a consistent hash or assigned ID) rather than truncation, so duplicate-checking still works reliably.

**5. Keep a stable internal ID for each course, even if the display name changes.**
Cohort 10 introduced course names that may or may not be the same as Cohort 9's, there was no way to tell from the data alone. If a course is renamed or repositioned between cohorts, keep a consistent internal reference so a genuine rename doesn't look like a totally different course in future analysis.

**6. Log the registration source (campaign, post, or link) alongside the channel.**
The spikes found in this analysis were real and not a data problem, but distinguishing organic growth from one campaign that happened to work would be far easier with a campaign or UTM-style tag on the registration link, rather than reconstructing it after the fact from timestamps.
